## 1. Theoretical Background and Mathematical Modeling

System identification provides a mathematical representation of a physical system that can be used to predict its behavior and support controller design.

For the QUBE-Servo 3, the system can be modeled from the fundamental electrical and mechanical dynamics of its DC motor.

### 1.1 Armature Circuit Dynamics

The motor armature voltage is described by:

$$
V_m(t) = R_m i_m(t) + e_b(t)
$$

Where:

* $V_m(t)$ = motor armature voltage
* $R_m$ = armature resistance
* $i_m(t)$ = armature current
* $e_b(t)$ = back-EMF voltage

The back-EMF is proportional to the motor's angular velocity:

$$
e_b(t) = K_b \omega_m(t)
$$

Where $K_b$ is the back-EMF constant and $\omega_m(t)$ is the motor angular velocity.

Substituting the back-EMF relationship into the armature equation gives:

$$
V_m(t) = R_m i_m(t) + K_b \omega_m(t)
$$

### 1.2 Electromagnetic Torque

The torque generated by the DC motor is proportional to the armature current:

$$
T_m(t) = K_t i_m(t)
$$

Where $K_t$ is the motor torque constant.

Therefore, the armature current can be expressed as:

$$
i_m(t) = \frac{T_m(t)}{K_t}
$$

### 1.3 Mechanical Dynamics

Applying Newton's second law for rotational motion gives:

$$
T_{\text{net}} = J_{\text{eq}} \dot{\omega}_m(t)
$$

The net torque acting on the system is:

$$
T_{\text{net}} = T_m(t) - T_{\text{app}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
J_{\text{eq}} \dot{\omega}_m(t)
=
T_m(t) - T_{\text{app}}
$$

The equivalent inertia is represented by:

$$
J_{\text{eq}} = J_m + J_l + J_d
$$

Where:

* $J_m$ = motor inertia
* $J_l$ = load inertia
* $J_d$ = disk inertia
* $T_{\text{app}}$ = applied opposing/load torque

This equation describes the mechanical dynamics of the motor and its attached load.

## 2. Transfer Function Derivation

The transfer functions were obtained by applying the Laplace transform under zero initial conditions:

$$
i_m(0^-) = 0,
\qquad
\omega_m(0^-) = 0
$$

### 2.1 Voltage-to-Speed Transfer Function

The motor's electrical and mechanical equations can be combined to obtain a first-order relationship between motor voltage and angular velocity.

The resulting voltage-to-speed transfer function is:

$$
\frac{\Omega_m(s)}{V_m(s)}
=
\frac{K_m}{\tau s + 1}
$$

Where:

* $K_m$ = motor speed gain
* $\tau$ = motor time constant

This model indicates that the motor speed behaves approximately as a first-order system.

### 2.2 Voltage-to-Position Transfer Function

Motor angular velocity and angular position are related by:

$$
\omega_m(t) = \frac{d\theta_m(t)}{dt}
$$

In the Laplace domain:

$$
\Omega_m(s) = s\Theta_m(s)
$$

Hence:

$$
\Theta_m(s) = \frac{\Omega_m(s)}{s}
$$

Multiplying the voltage-to-speed transfer function by the integrator $1/s$ gives the voltage-to-position model:

$$
G(s)
=
\frac{\Theta_m(s)}{V_m(s)}
=
\frac{K}{s(\tau s + 1)}
$$

The integrator represents the physical relationship between velocity and position.

## 3. Experimental System Identification

### 3.1 Experimental Configuration

A step-response experiment was performed using the QUBE-Servo 3 under a closed-loop PD control configuration with unity feedback.

The purpose was to extract the parameters of the simplified model:

$$
G(s) = \frac{K}{s(\tau s + 1)}
$$

from measured experimental data.

### 3.2 Test Input

A $1.0$ V step command was applied at:

$$
t_{\text{start}} = 1.0 \text{ s}
$$

The system response was monitored over the duration of the experiment.

### 3.3 Steady-State Position and Motor Gain

At $t = 5.0$ s, the measured steady-state position was:

$$
\theta_{ss} = 0.1451 \text{ rad}
$$

For a $1.0$ V input, the experimentally identified gain was calculated as:

$$
K = \frac{\theta_{ss}}{V_m}
$$

Substituting the measured values:

$$
K = \frac{0.1451}{1.0}
= 0.1451 \text{ rad/(V}\cdot\text{s)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{K = 0.1451}
$$

### 3.4 Time Constant

For a first-order response, the time constant corresponds approximately to the point at which the response reaches $63.2%$ of its final value.

The target position is:

$$
\theta_{63.2\%} = 0.632(0.1451)
$$

Which gives:

$$
\theta_{63.2\%} \approx 0.0917 \text{ rad}
$$

From the experimental data, the response reached approximately this value at:

$$
t_{63} = 1.7240 \text{ s}
$$

The time constant is therefore:

$$
\tau = t_{63} - t_{\text{start}}
$$

$$
\tau = 1.7240 - 1.0000
$$

$$
\boxed{\tau = 0.7240 \text{ s}}
$$

The corresponding experimental data point was identified around Row 863, where the measured position was approximately $0.0924$ rad.

## 4. Identified Experimental Model

Using the experimentally determined parameters:

$$
K = 0.1451
$$

$$
\tau = 0.7240 \text{ s}
$$

The identified voltage-to-position model is:

$$
\boxed{
G(s) =
\frac{0.1451}{s(0.7240s + 1)}
}
$$

This model provides a simplified mathematical representation of the measured QUBE-Servo 3 dynamics.

The model contains:

* **An integrator, $1/s$:** Represents the relationship between angular velocity and position.
* **A first-order dynamic term, $1/(0.7240s + 1)$:** Represents the motor's dominant dynamic response.
* **A gain of $0.1451$:** Relates the applied voltage to the resulting position response.

## 5. Real-World Non-Ideal Behaviors

The experimental response demonstrated several behaviors that are not fully captured by the idealized transfer-function model.

### 5.1 Derivative Kick

A significant transient was observed immediately after the reference step at $t = 1.0$ s.

Because the PD controller responds to the derivative of the error, an abrupt change in the reference produces a very large derivative component.

The controller output is represented conceptually as:

$$
u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt}
$$

When the reference changes rapidly, the error derivative can become very large, resulting in a large controller output.

In the experimental data:

* The controller voltage reached approximately $3.7500$ V immediately after the step.
* The voltage subsequently swung to approximately $-1.1756$ V during the transient.

This behavior is commonly referred to as **derivative kick**.

The observed sequence can be summarized as follows:

1. The reference position changes abruptly.
2. The tracking error changes rapidly.
3. The derivative term responds strongly to this change.
4. A large control-voltage transient is produced.
5. The controller subsequently applies an opposing voltage as the system responds.
6. The motor exhibits rapid transient fluctuations before settling.

This demonstrates why practical PD/PID implementations often require derivative filtering or alternative derivative implementations.

### 5.2 Closed-Loop Versus Open-Loop Dynamics

The experimentally measured response was obtained within a closed-loop control system, rather than by simply applying an open-loop voltage directly to the motor.

For a general feedback system, the closed-loop transfer function is:

$$
\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}
=
\frac{C(s)P(s)}{1 + C(s)P(s)}
$$

Where:

* $C(s)$ = controller transfer function
* $P(s)$ = plant transfer function
* $R(s)$ = reference input
* $Y(s)$ = system output

Consequently, the measured response is influenced by both the physical plant and the controller.

This distinction is important when interpreting experimentally identified parameters: a response measured under closed-loop control may not represent the plant dynamics alone.

### 5.3 Hardware Saturation

The physical amplifier and actuator have finite voltage and power capabilities.

When the controller demands an output beyond the available hardware range, the actuator saturates.

Conceptually:

$$
u_{\text{actual}} = \operatorname{sat}(u_{\text{controller}})
$$

Therefore, even if the controller mathematically requests a larger voltage, the hardware can only deliver a limited value.

Saturation can introduce:

* Slower-than-expected responses
* Increased settling time
* Distortion of the ideal step response
* Large transient errors
* Differences between theoretical and experimental behavior

This is an important consideration when comparing the identified model with the physical system.

## 6. Frequency Response

The identified model can also be examined in the frequency domain.

For the first-order component:

$$
G(s) = \frac{K}{\tau s + 1}
$$

Substituting $s = j\omega$ gives:

$$
G(j\omega) = \frac{K}{1 + j\tau\omega}
$$

The magnitude response is:

$$
|G(j\omega)|
=
\frac{K}{\sqrt{1 + (\tau\omega)^2}}
$$

This relationship shows how the motor's response changes with input frequency.

### 6.1 Cutoff Frequency

The characteristic or cutoff frequency of the first-order component is approximately:

$$
\omega_c = \frac{1}{\tau}
$$

Using the experimentally identified time constant:

$$
\omega_c = \frac{1}{0.7240}
\approx 1.38 \text{ rad/s}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\omega_c \approx 1.38 \text{ rad/s}}
$$

At frequencies significantly above this value, the motor's response magnitude decreases, indicating that the physical system has increasing difficulty following rapidly changing inputs.

---

## 7. Closed-Loop Control Relationships

For a feedback control system consisting of controller $C(s)$ and plant $P(s)$, the closed-loop reference-to-output transfer function is:

$$
T(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}
= \frac{C(s)P(s)}{1 + C(s)P(s)}
$$

This is also referred to as the **complementary sensitivity function**.

The corresponding sensitivity function is:

$$
S(s) = \frac{1}{1 + C(s)P(s)}
$$

The tracking error is therefore:

$$
E(s) = \frac{R(s)}{1 + C(s)P(s)}
$$

These relationships form the basis for evaluating tracking performance, disturbance rejection, and the effect of controller design on the physical plant.
